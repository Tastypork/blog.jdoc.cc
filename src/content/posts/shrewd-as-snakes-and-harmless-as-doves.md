Shrewd as snakes and harmless as doves. Keishon, who goes by Keish, led this one on November 24, and he took the title from Jesus' words in [Matthew 10:16 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Matthew+10:16/). It was a little different from the studies he usually leads. Those are typically chapter-based. This one was topical, under the heading of not having a blind faith. Scripture, he said, doesn't call us to a blind faith. [1 Peter 3:15 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Peter+3:15/) tells us to be [“always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Peter+3:15/). And in [Isaiah 1:18 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Isaiah+1:18/), God invites his people to reason with him: [“Come now, let us reason together, says the LORD” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Isaiah+1:18/).

We all have experiences, feelings, and things God has revealed to us. Keishon wanted us to reason out our belief and have a defense for why we believe. He planned to make a case for why God has to exist, then face some of the harder counterarguments. Hard questions, he said, come with nuance, and not everybody is in the same place. Share them in love.

<h2 style="font-size: 1.1875rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">What we discussed</h2>

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">I AM and the unmoved mover</h3>

We started in [Exodus 3:14 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Exodus+3:14/). God says to Moses, [“I AM WHO I AM” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Exodus+3:14/). In the Hebrew that's *ehyeh asher ehyeh*, which can be translated "I AM WHO I AM" or "I will be." And he tells Moses to say to the Israelites, [“I AM has sent me to you” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Exodus+3:14/). Keishon said "I AM" in this case expresses ultimate self-sufficiency. It's easy to gloss over when we read Exodus, but in the ancient world it is a very in-depth explanation, in so few words, of the need for God to exist. Of course God said it. He knows how to describe himself.

From there he moved to the argument St. Thomas called the unmoved mover, the first of Aquinas' five ways for God's existence, building on Aristotle. He asked us to follow it step by step, because it can get confusing.

First, change exists. Change here means something going from potential to actual. Something can have the potential to be loud while it is actually quiet. Change is the transition from quietness to loudness. Next, things that change are being provided that change through something else. A pot of cold water has the potential to be hot, but it is actually cold, and it doesn't have the capability to actualize itself into hot water. Something else has to act on it.

Last, things that are dependent on something else for their change cannot make up a chain with no independent source of power. The series has to terminate in a first. Keishon used an extension cord. Can you plug an extension cord into another extension cord, and that one into another, and another? Will you ever get a charge? No. To have an electrical charge, there needs to be an actual source. It has to terminate in the first. For the causal series of the universe to exist, there needs to be something that doesn't derive its actuality from something else. It must be purely actual. God is purely actual. He doesn't have any potential. He is the source.

When you look back at Exodus 3 and God's description of himself as "I AM," you can see the connection. He isn't just another thing in the universe. He is the foundation. Keishon was careful to say this reasoning doesn't prove the existence of the Christian God. It helps us reason to the necessity of God. If you were talking to an atheist, the point would be that God has to exist. Whether you believe in Christ is a different question. There are more steps in Aquinas' argument, and in the wider cosmological argument, if you want them. This was the first one, and it was enough to get us started.

> **He isn't just another thing in the universe. He is the foundation.**

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">The case for Christ</h3>

That bare-bones case for God existing still doesn't give you a reason why Christianity is true. So Keishon turned to the basic facts apologists use to argue for the case of Christ, including some that various secular scholars, Bart Ehrman among them, also take as historically solid. Jesus was crucified, a fact backed by the Bible and by history. He was influential enough to make disciples. Those disciples fully believed they had seen the risen Jesus, and believed it to the point of death. And then a third point, less historically guaranteed by everybody: Jesus' body was not found.

There's a difference, Keishon said, between dying for something you believe is true and dying for something you know is false. What person would steal a body to promote a fake religion, and then die for it? It doesn't really make much sense. If the disciples genuinely believed they encountered the risen Jesus, their willingness to suffer for that belief tells us a lot. There's strong evidence for Peter, Paul, and James, though people like Ehrman challenge the integrity of the others.

The question he put to the group was whether the disciples could have been mistaken. Grief came up, and the idea of a mass hysteria, or of people imagining they had seen him. Pride was another possibility: once you've said it, you don't want to look like a fool, so you keep the story going. The group didn't find those explanations very strong. Someone asked how that would explain Jesus' enemies also turning to him, especially Paul, who had been killing Christians. Guilt was offered as a possible pressure in Paul's case, but even then, going all the way to martyrdom would take a kind of conviction that those alternatives don't really account for. Other religions and later movements came up too, as a caution against treating any lasting story as automatically true. Still, the group kept coming back to this: the most plausible explanation for a whole faith being built on that claim, by people who had been fishermen before it, is that it is true.

Someone also pointed past the disciples to the rest of Scripture, to the way Jesus is already being depicted in the Old Testament, from the first sacrifices to Abraham and Isaac, and to the Dead Sea Scrolls matching what we have now, so that the text wasn't later changed to fit.

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">Why God allows suffering</h3>

Then came a hard question. Why would an all-loving God allow suffering? The group didn't treat it as simple. One answer was that if suffering never existed, it would be really hard for us to depend on something other than ourselves. The pushback was just as serious. How does that help a child who has no mother, is born only into suffering, never knows anything else, and then dies? The group didn't pretend to have a complete answer. Someone said God is beyond our comprehension, and that he can still reach a person even when we don't see how. Someone else said this life is a blink of an eye, which doesn't erase the pain, but it does put it in a larger frame.

Free will became the center of it. Suffering isn't from him inherently. He allows it because he loves us enough to give us the ability to choose. Keishon agreed, with the caveat that he isn't God and God knows all. In order for us to have free will, suffering had to be allowed, because God knew some of us were going to be evil, and he let that run its course in human hearts because we chose it. The beautiful thing, the group said, is that we are not alone in it. Our God suffered. We didn't relate to Jesus by suffering. He related to us by suffering. That's [Hebrews 4:15 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Hebrews+4:15/): [“we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Hebrews+4:15/).

The group also talked about how much of what we call suffering is the product of our own deeds, and that suffering can be a matter of perspective, though that's a hard point to make when the pain is real.

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">Grace, free will, and opportunity</h3>

The next question was just as hard. Does everyone get the same opportunity to come to repentance? The group was not of one mind.

Someone brought up Jesus' words about the cities that saw his miracles and still did not repent. If the same mighty works had been done in Sodom, as Jesus says in [Matthew 11:21-24 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Matthew+11:21-24/), they would have repented, and [“it will be more tolerable on the day of judgment for the land of Sodom than for you” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Matthew+11:24/). The Lord himself, the group noted, speaks as if people do not all get the same opportunity. Keishon set that next to [2 Peter 3:9 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/2+Peter+3:9/): [“The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/2+Peter+3:9/).

How do those sit together? One reading offered in the room was that "all" means everyone who was going to come to repentance. Someone brought up the parable of the wheat and the weeds in [Matthew 13:24-30 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Matthew+13:24-30/). The enemy sows weeds among the wheat. The servants want to pull them up, and the master says no, [“lest in gathering the weeds you root up the wheat along with them” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Matthew+13:29/). Let both grow together until the harvest. In that picture, God is patient in the wheat's development. He doesn't want the wheat rooted up.

The tension stayed with us. [1 Timothy 2:4 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Timothy+2:4/) says God [“desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Timothy+2:4/). Keishon's own understanding, as he put it that night, is that God desires all to receive his grace, but because there is free will there is also cooperation. We can reject it. He didn't want to box God into "he just decides, and that's it." The group kept laying verses beside each other. [Titus 2:11 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Titus+2:11/): [“the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Titus+2:11/). [1 Corinthians 15:22 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+15:22/): [“in Christ shall all be made alive” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+15:22/). [John 6:40 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/John+6:40/): [“this is the will of my Father, that everyone who looks on the Son and believes in him should have eternal life” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/John+6:40/).

Foreknowledge and predestination came up. If God already knows who will reject him, is the offer still real? Someone used Judas as an example. He still had the privileges of a disciple. He was still given power, still walked with Jesus, and he was still going to betray him. The gift is held out. We accept it or we don't. God can give the opportunity and still know that a person is going to reject it.

Keishon came back to the cities, and then to [Romans 2:14-16 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Romans+2:14-16/). Gentiles who do not have the law [“by nature do what the law requires” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Romans+2:14/). They show that [“the work of the law is written on their hearts, while their conscience also bears witness” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Romans+2:15/), and this will take place [“on that day when, according to my gospel, God judges the secrets of men by Christ Jesus” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Romans+2:16/). So does God give everybody the same opportunity to come to repentance? Keishon said he would personally say no, but a fair judgment, he would say yes. The opportunities may not be equal. God is still just.

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">We've been given much</h3>

That led into a last question. Do we, as Americans, have a better chance of going to heaven than people in other countries? The group was careful to separate exposure from the chance of heaven. Some of us have more access, more freedom to hear and to speak, more open talk about Christ. That is not the same thing as a better seat in the judgment.

Someone pointed out that God is able to make himself known even in places that were isolated from the early church, and that he can speak to people in their own language and their own understanding, even without the Bible in their hands. Others noted that more exposure can also mean more distraction, and that a person discovering Christ in a harder place may be fighting to get to him, while it's easy here to say we'll talk to God tomorrow. Keishon pressed the point. We've been given a lot: freedom to speak, room to live our faith openly, easy access to the Lord. And still we often don't want to talk to the neighbor, don't want to evangelize, don't want to show them Christlike love, while other believers take great risks just to gather and seek him.

He landed on that familiar line from [Luke 12:48 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Luke+12:48/): [“Everyone to whom much was given, of him much will be required” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Luke+12:48/). He didn't think that means we have a better chance at heaven, even if there's more opportunity for repentance here. God wishes that all come, and "all" doesn't mean Americans. It means all people. God is just, and he will judge people accordingly.

> **Everyone to whom much was given, of him much will be required.**
