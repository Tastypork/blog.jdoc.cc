Faithfulness and humility. Ashley led this one on November 17, a study of 1 Corinthians 4 on what it truly means to serve Christ. Her approach was a standard, careful Bible study: understand what God was saying through Paul to the church in Corinth, then ask how that truth applies to us now. For each section she used three questions. What does the text say? What did it mean to the original audience? How should we respond to it today?

<h2 style="font-size: 1.1875rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">What we discussed</h2>

Before reading the chapter, Ashley set up why Paul was writing. The Corinthian church was divided. People were choosing sides and lifting up leaders, some preferring Paul, some Apollos, and some Cephas. Instead of seeing these men as servants God had used, they treated them almost like competing personalities. So Paul corrects their picture of Christian leadership. Ashley pointed out that he isn't only talking to pastors and leaders. He's addressing the attitude of the whole church, so she asked us to watch for where those same attitudes show up in our own lives.

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">Servants and stewards</h3>

We started with [1 Corinthians 4:1-5 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:1-5/). Paul describes himself and Apollos [“as servants of Christ and stewards of the mysteries of God” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:1/). Ashley explained that a steward was someone entrusted with managing something that belonged to another person. Ministry belongs to God. Leaders are entrusted with responsibility, but they are not owners. And the one requirement Paul names is faithfulness: [“it is required of stewards that they be found faithful” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:2/).

That reaches past church leadership. Raising kids, working a job, serving your family, encouraging someone. God has entrusted each of us with responsibilities, and the question is whether we're being faithful with what he's given us.

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">The Lord is the judge</h3>

In verses 3 through 5, Paul explains that human opinion is not the final standard. [“It is the Lord who judges me” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:4/), and he will [“disclose the purposes of the heart” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:5/). God sees what people can't, including motives and intentions.

Ashley tied this to something she sees a lot, especially on social media, where people go as far as proclaiming who is saved and who isn't. There is such a thing as righteous judgment, but this passage warns against setting ourselves up as the final word. That doesn't mean we never use discernment or hold people accountable. It means final judgment belongs to God, not us. Her question for this section: are we more concerned about being recognized by people, or about being faithful to God?

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">What do you have that you did not receive?</h3>

Next Ashley asked what could destroy that kind of stewardship, and the group landed on pride. That's where [1 Corinthians 4:6-7 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:6-7/) goes. Paul wants them to learn [“not to go beyond what is written, that none of you may be puffed up in favor of one against another” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:6/). Then he asks three questions, including [“What do you have that you did not receive?” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:7/)

Ashley loves these questions because they force us to examine ourselves. Our talents, our opportunities, our spiritual gifts, even our understanding of God's word are all gifts from God, and sometimes we get proud of the very things he gave us. We compare ministries. We compare spiritual maturity and decide someone else is still a baby in the faith. Even if that's true, she said, we aren't the ones who should be saying it. God has a way of quickly reminding us we are no better than anybody else. Grace should produce gratitude, not arrogance.

The group talked about how subtle pride can be. Being good at something and quietly thinking "I did that." Having been somewhere longer and knowing the ropes. A few people shared how God put them right in the areas they resisted, and how serving there broke down their pride. We also talked about blind spots and unconscious bias, and what humility looks like in our relationships. The answers that came up were patience, the ability to understand someone else's perspective, and dying to yourself.

> **Grace should produce gratitude, not arrogance.**

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">Fools for Christ</h3>

In [1 Corinthians 4:8-13 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:8-13/), Paul turns to irony. [“Already you have all you want! Already you have become rich!” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:8/) He's using sarcasm to confront the Corinthians' self-image, their illusion of spiritual greatness. Then he contrasts it with the apostles: [“We are fools for Christ’s sake, but you are wise in Christ” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:10/). The apostles were hungry, poorly dressed, homeless, and working with their own hands. And how did they respond? [“When reviled, we bless; when persecuted, we endure; when slandered, we entreat” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:12-13/).

Ashley pointed out how counter that is to what our culture tells us, which is an eye for an eye. If something is wrong between you and a brother or sister, you should go talk about it. But we often go a step further, holding it against them or spreading it to other people. Christian maturity, she said, isn't measured by status, comfort, or recognition. It shows in what we do when someone is trying to upset us or treat us unfairly.

The group was honest that this is one of the hardest parts of following Jesus. One person talked about holding resentment for weeks after being lied about, and how it ended up poisoning them more than anyone else. Another shared trying to learn when to speak and when to hold their tongue, and talking to God about it either way. Ashley noted that responding like the apostles is not weakness. Jesus said the meek are blessed, [“for they shall inherit the earth” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/Matthew+5:5/). This is Christlike character. Following Jesus doesn't mean we'll never face difficulty, so the real test isn't how we act when life is good. It's how we respond when things get hard.

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">Be imitators</h3>

The last stretch was [1 Corinthians 4:14-21 (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:14-21/). Paul says he isn't writing to shame them [“but to admonish you as my beloved children” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:14/). He became their spiritual father through the gospel, and he urges them, [“be imitators of me” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:16/), sending Timothy to remind them of his ways in Christ.

Ashley found that one challenging. Would we want someone to imitate our attitude? The way we talk, the way we handle conflict, the way we come home after a hard day? Paul understood that spiritual leadership isn't only teaching the truth. It's living it.

That leads to verse 20: [“the kingdom of God does not consist in talk but in power” (ESV)](https://www.esv.org/1+Corinthians+4:20/). Paul is challenging people who are arrogant in their words but don't show the reality of God's kingdom in their lives. The kingdom isn't just what we say. It's God's active, transforming work. Our faith should produce transformation, and people, both those following Christ and those who aren't, should see Jesus in the way we live. That turned into a good conversation about sharing the gospel, and most of the group leaned toward living it out and having real, relational conversations over forcing it on people.

Ashley closed the section with one more application question: are we teachable when someone corrects us? Correction hurts, the group agreed, but when it comes in love it's usually there for a reason. Who it comes from and how it's said matter too.

> **God is not asking us to compete with one another. He's calling us to be faithful.**

<h3 style="font-size: 1.05rem; font-weight: 600; line-height: 1.35;">Stepping back</h3>

Looking over the whole chapter, Ashley summed up what Paul was really addressing: pride, comparison, and the desire for recognition. Everything comes from God. Leaders are servants. And genuine spiritual maturity looks like humility and faithfulness. Her shorthand for it: whatever your pride is about to do, do the opposite.

She left us with a few questions to carry. Am I seeking God's approval? Am I grateful for what God has entrusted to me? And her last word was this. God is calling us to faithfully use what he's placed in our hands, to be people humble enough to receive correction, grateful enough to recognize God's grace, and faithful enough to serve even when nobody's watching.
